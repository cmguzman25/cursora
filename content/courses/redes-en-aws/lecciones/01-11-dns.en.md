# 1.11 — DNS: from the name to the number

## 🎯 What you'll understand today

How a name is turned into an address, and who does that conversion. You are
going to understand why a DNS change takes hours to show up. And you are going
to learn to separate two faults that always get confused: "it cannot find the
name" and "it finds it and does not connect".

## 📐 The idea

Machines understand each other with numbers. We people cannot remember them.
That is the second question from the start, and the reason DNS exists.

**DNS** (Domain Name System) is the internet's phone book. You give it a name
and it gives you back an address.

What is surprising is that this book **is nowhere**. There is no server with the
complete list, because it would be impossible to maintain and to query. Instead,
the information is spread out, and finding it is a chain of questions.

When your machine wants to know a name's address, it asks an intermediary. That
intermediary is the one who walks the chain:

```text
   your machine
       │  what is the address of tienda.ejemplo.com?
       ▼
   the intermediary
       │  asks about the end of the name and works down
       ├──→  who knows about .com?           → there you go
       ├──→  who knows about ejemplo.com?    → there you go
       └──→  and tienda.ejemplo.com?         → 203.0.113.40
       │
       ▼
   your machine can now open the connection
```

Notice that the name is read **from right to left**. First `.com` is resolved,
then `ejemplo.com`, and last the specific part. Each level knows who to ask at
the next one, and none of them knows everything.

And notice something more important. When it finishes, your machine has a
number. Nothing more. **DNS connects nothing**: it only translates.

## 🔬 In detail

Walking that whole chain for every query would be painfully slow. That is why
the whole system leans on keeping answers for a while.

Every answer comes with a **TTL** (Time To Live). It is a number of seconds
meaning "you may keep this for this long". Until it expires, nobody asks again.

There is the third question from the start. When you change where a name points,
the change is not immediate for anybody. Those who had the answer stored keep
going to the old place until theirs expires.

That is why there is a custom worth knowing. Before moving something, the TTL is
lowered to a few minutes and you wait. Then the change is made, which now
propagates quickly. And at the end it is raised again. Lowering it on the day of
the change is no use: nobody has found out yet.

Names are kept in **records**, and there are several types. Three will get you
by:

| Type | What it returns | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `A` | An address | `tienda.ejemplo.com` → `203.0.113.40` |
| `CNAME` | Another name, to ask again | `www.ejemplo.com` → `tienda.ejemplo.com` |
| `MX` | Where the domain's mail goes | `ejemplo.com` → mail server |

A `CNAME` is a forward. It says "what you are looking for is under this other
name, ask again". It is convenient for not repeating addresses in twenty places,
and it costs one more query.

That convenience has an advantage you appreciate on the day of the change. If
twenty names point by `CNAME` at a single one, moving the service means changing
**one** address. If each one carried its own `A` record, all twenty would have
to be touched, and forgetting one is the norm.

Here the phone book analogy stops working, and the detail explains a lot of
scares. A book is a book, and everybody reads the same one. DNS is spread across
copies that expire at different rates. Two people can ask the same thing at the
same instant and receive different answers.

## 🗺️ Where this shows up in AWS

Your VPC comes with its own intermediary, and it works without you doing
anything. It lives two positions above the start of the VPC's range. If your VPC
is `10.0.0.0/16`, it is at `10.0.0.2`, and that is why that address is reserved.
You will see it in lesson 2.5.

That intermediary does two jobs:

- Resolving internet names, like any other.
- Resolving the **internal** names of your own machines, which do not exist
  outside your network.

The second is the interesting one. You can create a **private zone**, which is a
phone book that only exists inside your VPC. There you define names like
`base-de-datos.interno`, pointing at private addresses from lesson 1.8. From the
internet, that name does not exist. From inside, it works.

The service that manages all of this in AWS is called **Route 53**, and it has
its own lesson, 7.5. The name comes from port 53, which is DNS's.

## ⚠️ Don't confuse it with

**Resolving with connecting.** This is the pair that always has to be separated.
A name resolving means the translation exists. It does not mean the machine is
switched on, or that there is a route. Nor that the firewall from lesson 1.12
lets you through. It is the first cut of any diagnosis: is the translation
failing or is the path failing?

**DNS with the company directory.** DNS does not know who you are and does not
check permissions. It answers anybody who asks. It is a public book, not an
access control.

**A change made with a change visible.** Because of the TTL, the two moments are
separate. If you change something and "it does not work", sometimes the old one
is only expiring somewhere.

## 🔁 Check yourself

1. In what order is a name like `tienda.ejemplo.com` resolved?
2. What is the TTL and what does it cause?
3. Where does a VPC's DNS intermediary live?
4. A name resolves fine and the page does not load. What do you rule out?

**Answers:** One, from right to left. First `.com`, then `ejemplo.com`, and last
the specific part. Two, the length of time an answer may be kept. It causes
changes to take a while to be seen everywhere. Three, at the second address of
the VPC's range. Four, you rule out DNS. The translation works, so the problem is
in the route, in the permission, or in the destination program.

## 🎒 Something to think about

A company moves its website to a new server on a Monday morning. The TTL was set
to 24 hours. During that day, some customers see the new site and some the old
one, with no clear pattern. What do you think determines who sees which? And if
the two sites write to different databases, what problem shows up?
