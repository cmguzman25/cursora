# 1.9 — NAT: how many get out through a single door

> Module 1 · Networking without the cloud · Lesson 9 of 13 · ⏱️ 8 min read · 💚 Cost: $0

## 🎯 What you'll understand today

How a machine with a private address loads a web page, if the internet does not
accept its address. The answer is called NAT. By the end you will understand why
getting out from inside is no trouble. And why getting in from outside is not
possible. That asymmetry defines half the networks you are going to design.

## 🤔 Before we start

1. In lesson 1.5 we saw three reasons for splitting a network. Do you remember
   any of them?
2. In a hotel, which address does the receptionist put on your letter when they
   send it?
3. If a reply to that letter arrives, how does the receptionist know which room
   to take it to?

## 📐 The idea

The problem was laid out in the previous lesson. Your machine is
`192.168.1.10`, and the internet discards everything going to that address. You
put a packet on the path and the reply cannot come back.

The answer is for somebody to lie on your behalf, carefully.

When your packet reaches the device at the border, that device **changes its
source address**. It erases your `192.168.1.10` and puts its own public one in.
The destination server sees a perfectly valid address, and replies there.

That translation is called **NAT** (Network Address Translation).

Now comes the interesting part. When the reply arrives, the device has to know
whose it was. And for that it **writes every translation down in a table**:

| Who went out | Public address it put in | Number |
|---|---|---|
| `192.168.1.10` | `203.0.113.7` | 51200 |
| `192.168.1.22` | `203.0.113.7` | 51201 |
| `192.168.1.10` | `203.0.113.7` | 51202 |

There are the questions from the start. The receptionist sends the letter with
the hotel's address, and writes down in a notebook which room sent it. When the
reply arrives, they check the notebook and take it up.

Notice the third column: they all share the same public address and are told
apart by a number. That number is a **port**, and lesson 1.10 is
entirely about it. For now just take it as the room number.

## 🔬 In detail

What really matters today is a consequence a lot of people rely on without
knowing it exists.

**The table is only written on the way out.**

When your machine sends something, a row is created. When the reply comes back,
the device looks up that row and knows who to deliver to. All correct.

Now imagine somebody on the internet sends a packet without anybody having
asked for it. It reaches the device at the border. That device looks up in the
table who it belongs to, and **finds nothing**. There is no row saying which
internal machine was expecting that.

The packet cannot be delivered. It is not that it is forbidden: it is that there
is no information with which to deliver it. It is discarded.

```text
   FROM INSIDE TO OUTSIDE        FROM OUTSIDE TO INSIDE
                                 (without anybody asking)

   leaves home                   a packet arrives
   → the row is written          → its row is looked up
   → goes out translated         → it does not exist
   → the reply comes back        → there is nobody to give it to
   → the row is looked up        → it is discarded
   → it is found
   → it reaches your machine
```

That effect looks a great deal like a firewall, and it is worth not confusing
them. A firewall, which is lesson 1.12, **decides** not to let something
through. NAT does not even decide: it does not know who to deliver it to. The
result looks alike and the reason is different.

The distinction matters the day you do want things to come in. Then the row has
to be written by hand. Something like "whatever arrives at port 443 goes to this
machine inside". It is what people do when they open a port on their home device
to play online.

And that is where the hotel analogy stops working. A receptionist with a letter
and no clear addressee would ask around, or leave it on a counter. NAT has no
such initiative: if there is no row, it discards and that is that. That lack of
imagination is precisely what makes it useful.

## 🗺️ Where this shows up in AWS

In AWS this mechanism has its own service, the **NAT Gateway**, and you will use
it in lesson 2.10.

Three things worth knowing before you get there:

- It lives in a **public subnet** and serves the **private** ones. It sounds odd
  until you think about it: the translator has to be able to get out itself.
- It works in one direction only, from what you have just seen. The private
  machines go out to download updates, and nobody on the internet can start a
  connection towards them. That is exactly the property being sought.
- **It costs money.** It is one of the few AWS networking components billed per
  hour and per gigabyte. It is the most expensive resource in this course, and
  that is why it gets created and deleted in consecutive lessons.

That combination, out yes and in no, is the practical definition of a private
subnet in the cloud. It is not disconnected from the world: it is disconnected
in one direction.

## ⚠️ Don't confuse it with

**NAT with a firewall.** NAT discards what it does not know how to deliver. A
firewall discards what it decides not to allow. The first is a side effect, the
second is a policy. Trusting NAT as protection works until somebody adds a
route.

**NAT Gateway with Internet Gateway.** They sound alike and they do different
things. The Internet Gateway is the VPC's door, and it lets things through in
both directions. The NAT Gateway translates so machines with no public address
can get out, and it only works outwards.

**Having NAT with having a way out.** NAT translates, but the packet still needs
a route to carry it there. Both things have to be present, and they fail
separately.

## 🔁 Check yourself

1. What exactly does NAT change in a packet going out?
2. What is the translation table for?
3. Why can a connection not be started from outside towards a machine behind
   NAT?
4. What kind of subnet does a NAT Gateway live in, and which kind does it serve?

**Answers:** One, the source address. It puts its own public one in place of your
machine's private one. Two, to know who each returning reply belongs to. Three,
because the table is only written on the way out. With no prior row, there is no
way of knowing which internal machine to deliver to. Four, it lives in a public
subnet and serves the private ones.

## 🎒 Something to think about

Two people in your home open the same web page at once. Both go out with the
same public address. Two almost identical replies arrive from the same server.
What piece of data has to be different for each one to reach its owner? Look at
the table above again.
